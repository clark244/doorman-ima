Key Findings
1
The central mechanism can be tested
The link between product use to distraction reduction, recovered instructional time, lasting digital habits, and behavioral referrals has not been formally measured. The habit formation claim that differentiates Doorman from pouches is the most commercially important.
2
Doorman already has impact measurement data
System logs capture blocked app attempts, focused minutes, compliance rates, and bypass events at the individual student level. These data streams have high potential to be aggregated, trended, and reported systematically. The gap is not data collection — it is data infrastructure.
3
The highest-value metrics require the lowest engineering effort
Year-over-year reduction in phone-related disciplinary referrals, attendance compliance gains from automated tracking, and instructional time recovered are all derivable from existing data or simple pre/post comparison. These are the specific evidence points buyers are requesting.
4
First-mover advantage on evidence is real and time-limited
No phone management competitor — including Yondr pouches — publishes systematic outcome data. Doorman can establish the category’s evidence baseline with a quarterly aggregated impact report built from data it already captures. This is a defensible commercial asset, but the window closes as the market matures.
01

About This Report

This report presents a working model of the mechanisms through which Doorman’s phone management platform produces impact — from product design through student compliance behavior to intended outcomes — and uses that model to identify and prioritize opportunities for impact measurement. The model is the analytical foundation. The measurement priorities follow directly from it.

The analysis draws on four sources: discovery conversations with Doorman’s founder Patrick Cui on May 7 and June 1, 2026; the Reach Capital investment memo; publicly available information at doorman.school; and Cobalt Collective’s framework for evaluating impact measurement readiness in education technology. It is a first-pass working draft, not a final assessment. We expect it to sharpen as Doorman shares more about its product data, internal metrics, and strategic priorities.

Our aim is not to evaluate Doorman’s impact, which early evidence suggests is meaningful, but to help Doorman’s leadership see clearly where the causal chain is well understood, where it is assumed but untested, and where the most important measurement investments should go — in what order, and why.

Proximal: reduced phone-related disruptions during class, fewer phone-related disciplinary referrals, teacher time recovered from automated attendance and reduced enforcement burden. Distal: student digital self-regulation — the ability to coexist productively with phones, developed through daily practice. Student outcomes are the primary impact claim; teacher and administrator experience improvements are important but secondary.

04

Impact Measurement Opportunities

Doorman is in an unusual position for an early-stage edtech company: it already captures rich behavioral data through its product — every tap-in, every blocked app attempt, every bypass event, every minute of focus mode — but does not yet treat this data as impact evidence. The measurement opportunity is not about building new data collection from scratch. It is about systematizing what Doorman already has and filling targeted gaps where existing data does not reach.

The priorities below are sequenced from lowest-effort/highest-value to higher-effort/longer-horizon. A critical strategic context: no phone management competitor — including Yondr pouches — publishes systematic outcome data. Doorman has a near-term window to establish the category’s evidence baseline. A quarterly aggregated impact report, built from data Doorman already captures, would be a defensible commercial asset. But the window closes as the market matures and competitors begin measuring their own impact.

Priorities

Measurement Priorities

The priorities below are sequenced: each one is more meaningful if the prior one is in place. All four can begin within the next 6–12 months, with the first two starting immediately. Summer 2026 is the optimal window for building the data infrastructure that will serve the 40–50 schools onboarding in Fall 2026.

1a Compliance & Engagement Data — The Behavioral Foundation Now Start Here ▼

This is the right place to start because it requires no new data collection — only systematization of what Doorman already captures. System logs already record every tap-in, every blocked app attempt, every bypass event, and every minute of focus mode at the individual student level. These data streams are currently retrieved ad hoc via database queries for individual renewal conversations. The opportunity is to aggregate, trend, and report them systematically — transforming operational logs into impact evidence.

What to measure

  • Daily tap-in compliance rate by school (percentage of enrolled students who activate Doorman per class period)
  • Blocked app attempts by category (social media, messaging, gaming) — aggregated weekly and monthly, normalized per student
  • Focused minutes generated per student per day, trended over time
  • Bypass attempt rate (VPN disconnection, app deletion, permission revocation) as a declining-over-time compliance indicator

This data is entirely internal and available now. A standardized per-school impact summary — generated automatically from system logs — could replace the current ad hoc data pulls for renewal conversations. Summer 2026 is the build window; Fall 2026 onboarding is the deployment deadline.

1b Disciplinary Referral Reduction — The Proximal Impact Metric Now–3 months Near-Term Evidence ▼

Phone-related disciplinary referrals are a commercially compelling metric Doorman can report. Schools already track disciplinary incidents in their SIS; Doorman automatically logs cell phone violations and writes them to the school’s system of record. The year-over-year comparison — pre-Doorman referral volume vs. post-Doorman — is straightforward to compute and powerfully persuasive in renewal and board-approval conversations.

This metric is slightly distal: it depends not only on whether Doorman reduces phone use during class, but on whether the school’s disciplinary system was capturing phone infractions systematically before Doorman. For schools that were — and many were, since phone conflicts were a top-two complaint — the pre/post comparison is credible. For schools that were not, Doorman’s own violation logs establish the baseline going forward.

What to measure

  • Year-over-year phone-related disciplinary referrals (pre-Doorman from school SIS records vs. Doorman-era automated tracking)
  • Phone-related referrals per 100 students per month, trended over the school year
  • Reduction in detentions and suspensions specifically tied to cell phone policy violations
  • Teacher-reported frequency of phone-related student conflicts (brief survey, pre/post comparison)

Begin by requesting pre-Doorman disciplinary referral data from each school during onboarding. Build this into the onboarding contract: “We will measure these metrics at 30/60/90 days.” Pre-registering the metrics prevents schools from later moving the goalposts and forces buy-in upfront.

1c Instructional Time Recovery & Teacher Experience 3–6 months Outcome Evidence ▼

Instructional time recovery is the metric that translates Doorman’s impact into dollars — and dollars are what district procurement officers and school boards understand. The logic is concrete: automated attendance saves teachers approximately 5 minutes per class period; reduced phone-related interruptions recover additional instructional minutes; at scale, this translates to measurable FTE-equivalent time savings per school.

Teacher experience is the complementary evidence point. EdSurge’s coverage of the Phones in Focus survey ran with a clear finding: the stricter the cellphone policy, the happier the teacher. Aligning Doorman’s teacher satisfaction instrument to the Phones in Focus survey structure would let Doorman claim third-party-grade credibility — “our schools score X points above the national average” — without conducting an independent study.

What to measure

  • Instructional time recovered per teacher per week (computed from attendance automation savings + reduced phone-related interruptions)
  • Teacher satisfaction with phone policy (5-question survey aligned to Phones in Focus instrument for external benchmarking)
  • Administrator time saved on phone-related discipline processing (estimated from referral volume reduction)

Recommendation: build a brief teacher experience survey (5–7 questions, Likert scale) and administer it at 30 and 90 days post-deployment. Align question wording to Phones in Focus where possible. Compute instructional time recovery using the attendance automation savings formula and a teacher-reported interruption tally during a one-week sampling window.

1d Student Habit Formation 6–12 months High Value, Longer Horizon ▼

The habit formation claim is Doorman’s core differentiator: the daily tap-in teaches students to “choose to be focused,” and keeping phones on-person (rather than confiscating them) builds resistance and coexistence skills for adult life. Measuring this requires data that Doorman does not currently collect — but could, with targeted infrastructure built during summer 2026.

What to measure

  • Student-reported focus and self-regulation (standardized survey administered via Doorman app, replacing ad hoc school-administered surveys)
  • Screen time usage during unrestricted school periods (lunch, breaks) — the most informative behavioral window, requires new device permissions
  • Bypass attempt trends over time per student (declining attempts = developing self-regulation)
  • Student attitudes toward phone use and focus (brief pulse questions delivered through the app notification system)

Priority 1d not because it matters less, but because the screen time permission requires new onboarding flows (especially on iOS) and the survey instrument requires careful design to get serious responses from 15–16-year-olds. Build the permission prompts into new school onboarding starting Fall 2026; design the survey instrument this summer. The declining-bypass-attempt metric is available now from existing logs and can begin immediately as a leading indicator.

05

Recommended Next Steps

The priorities above imply a specific set of near-term actions. Summer 2026 is the critical build window; Fall 2026 school onboarding is the deployment deadline. The first two actions can begin this week.

Immediate (this summer)
1
System log audit and reporting infrastructure. Map the data Doorman currently captures (tap-in events, blocked attempts, focused minutes, bypass events, violation logs) and build automated aggregation that produces a standardized per-school impact summary. Target: a one-page impact report that can be generated for any school on demand.
2
Pre-Doorman baseline collection protocol. Build a standard data request into the onboarding contract for new schools: prior-year phone-related disciplinary referrals, attendance compliance rates, and (if available) teacher satisfaction data. Pre-registering these metrics forces buy-in upfront.
3
Teacher experience survey design. Develop a brief (5–7 question) teacher satisfaction instrument aligned to the Phones in Focus survey structure where possible. Plan to administer at 30 and 90 days post-deployment for the Fall 2026 cohort.
4
Terms of service review for data use. Confirm that Doorman’s terms of service permit aggregate, de-identified use of system log data for impact reporting and product improvement. If not, update the terms before Fall onboarding begins.
Near-term (Fall 2026 school year)
5
Deploy standardized impact reporting. Generate the per-school impact summary at 30/60/90 days for all new schools. Use these reports in renewal conversations and pilot-to-contract conversions. Aggregate across schools for the first quarterly Doorman Impact Report — a category-defining sales asset.
6
Student focus survey pilot. Administer the standardized student survey via Doorman’s app notification system in 3–5 willing schools. Test question clarity and response seriousness with the target age group before broader rollout.
7
Screen time data infrastructure. For new schools onboarded in Fall 2026, include screen time permission prompts in the onboarding flow. Begin collecting usage data during unrestricted school periods (lunch, breaks) to build the behavioral foundation for the habit formation claim.
